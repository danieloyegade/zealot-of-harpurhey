CLAUDE CODE / BLENDER ASSET BRIEF
ZEALOT OF HARPERHAY — TWO WORN WOODEN PALLETS

OBJECTIVE
Create TWO separate, production-ready Blender assets and export each as its own .glb for use in the Three.js game Zealot of Harperhay:

1. pallet_worn_brown.glb — an unpainted/brown timber shipping pallet, old and heavily used.
2. pallet_worn_blue.glb — a blue-painted timber shipping pallet, old and heavily used, based closely on the supplied blue pallet reference photographs.

These are environmental props, not pristine product visualisations. They should feel like real pallets found behind shops, in alleys, loading bays and service yards in Manchester: industrial, handled repeatedly, left outside, scraped by forklifts, dirty, damp and imperfect.

REFERENCE IMAGES
Inspect all five supplied pallet photographs before modelling:
- /mnt/data/def.jpeg
- /mnt/data/2020-Blog-800x500-Jan-CHEP.jpg
- /mnt/data/pallet_original1.jpg
- /mnt/data/images(9).jpeg
- /mnt/data/wooden-pallet.jpg

Use the references primarily for:
- construction and proportions
- board thickness
- gaps between top boards
- block/stringer construction
- edge wear
- blue paint colour variation
- exposed timber beneath chipped paint
- dirt accumulation
- nail positions
- scratches and abrasions
- roughness variation
- the overall heavily-used industrial character

Do NOT simply project one photograph over the whole asset. Build proper UVs and materials.

ART DIRECTION
The game is moving toward an uncanny midpoint between photographic realism and a game-like constructed world. Therefore, these props should be convincing at close/medium distance without becoming excessively high-poly.

Avoid:
- clean procedural-looking wood
- perfectly straight pristine edges
- uniform blue paint
- smooth plastic surfaces
- identical duplicated boards
- exaggerated cartoon damage
- perfectly symmetrical dirt
- generic “wood grain” stretched across multiple boards
- excessive geometry that will not affect the silhouette

The biggest priority is MATERIAL AND SURFACE FIDELITY.

GEOMETRY
Model believable UK/European-style wooden shipping pallets. Approximately 1200 x 800 mm is an appropriate real-world starting footprint, but use the references as the visual authority.

Construct the pallet from separate believable wooden components rather than one monolithic box:
- top deck boards
- supporting boards/stringers
- blocks
- bottom deck boards

Maintain real gaps between boards.

Boards should NOT all be exact clones. Introduce restrained variation:
- tiny width/thickness differences
- subtle bowing or unevenness where appropriate
- slightly battered corners
- small chips/notches along selected edges
- minor dents
- occasional splintered areas

Keep damage plausible. The pallet still needs to read as structurally usable.

Use small bevels on exposed timber edges so highlights catch naturally. Do not leave razor-sharp CG edges.

Add visible nail heads/fasteners in plausible construction positions. These may use inexpensive geometry or another efficient method, but they should catch light and contribute to realism.

BROWN PALLET — MATERIAL
This must look like aged, unfinished timber rather than “brown coloured material.”

Create a physically plausible PBR wood surface with:
- visible longitudinal wood grain
- grain direction correctly aligned PER BOARD
- variation between individual planks
- pale raw timber in fresher abrasions
- greyed/weathered areas
- darker ingrained dirt
- subtle water staining
- darkened board ends/end grain
- compressed/scuffed areas
- occasional tiny dark marks around nails
- rough fibres and shallow scratches

The timber should contain several related brown/grey/tan values rather than a single base colour.

Important: randomise or offset texture coordinates per plank so repeated boards do not reveal obvious texture repetition.

BLUE PALLET — MATERIAL
This is the hero variation and should be particularly close to the references.

The pallet was originally painted a strong industrial blue, but the paint is now substantially weathered.

The blue surface MUST show layered material history:
BLUE PAINT → ABRASION/CHIPPING → EXPOSED WOOD → DIRT/WEATHERING.

Do not make a blue wood texture by simply tinting the brown pallet.

Required characteristics:
- saturated industrial blue remaining in protected areas
- faded/desaturated blue across exposed upper surfaces
- large irregular rubbed areas where paint has worn thin
- smaller chips revealing tan/brown timber
- especially strong wear on corners and board edges
- scratches running with and across the grain
- dirty dark areas in recesses and around fasteners
- subtle black/brown grime
- slight green/grey environmental staining in a few places if natural
- paint caught more strongly in lower/protected areas
- varied roughness between remaining paint, bare wood and dirt
- visible wood grain continuing beneath/through worn paint

Look carefully at the supplied photographs: some top boards have broad pale exposed strips and patches, while other areas retain much stronger blue. Reproduce this kind of irregular, history-driven distribution rather than generic noise.

TEXTURE AUTHORING
Prioritise texture quality over polygon density.

Use a Principled BSDF/PBR workflow suitable for glTF/GLB:
- Base Color
- Roughness
- Normal
- optional AO contribution where appropriate

Do not rely on Blender-only shader tricks that disappear during GLB export. Bake procedural effects to image textures if necessary.

Target 2K texture maps for each pallet/material set unless a strong technical reason justifies otherwise. Keep texture memory sensible for a browser-based Three.js game.

If practical, pack maps efficiently for glTF.

NORMAL / MICRODETAIL
Use normal detail for:
- shallow wood grain
- fine scratches
- small dents
- chipped paint transitions
- subtle rough fibres

Do NOT fake major silhouette damage only with a normal map. Conversely, do not model every scratch.

ROUGHNESS
This is crucial.

The object must NOT have one uniform roughness value.

Brown pallet:
- generally very rough/matte
- slightly different roughness on compressed/worn patches
- dirt and damp staining can subtly alter response

Blue pallet:
- old paint is mostly matte
- exposed wood is strongly rough
- heavily rubbed/compressed paint can have slightly smoother patches
- dirt/recesses should vary subtly

There should be no broad glossy/plastic sheen.

UV REQUIREMENTS
Create clean, non-overlapping UVs suitable for baked PBR textures.
Maintain appropriate texel density.
Orient wood grain correctly for every board.
Avoid obvious seams on the most visible upper surfaces.
Do not allow a single continuous grain pattern to unrealistically cross separate pieces of timber.

REALISM / IMPERFECTION PASS
After the basic asset is finished, deliberately perform a second pass asking:
“What makes this still look computer-generated?”

Correct:
- overly even spacing
- perfect edges
- repeated texture patches
- identical wear
- perfectly uniform colour
- pristine undersides
- insufficient dirt around joins
- lack of contact grime
- unnaturally clean nails
- excessive symmetry

Wear should be causally distributed:
- corners: collisions/chipping
- upper deck: foot/load abrasion and weather exposure
- fork openings/lower edges: forklift impacts and grime
- board joins: dirt accumulation
- nail heads: local darkening/rust staining where appropriate
- underside: dirt, but less sun bleaching than the top

OPTIMISATION
These assets are for Three.js and must be efficient.

Aim for a sensible environmental-prop budget, roughly:
- ~3,000–8,000 triangles per pallet preferred
- go slightly higher only if clearly justified by silhouette/detail
- avoid hidden unnecessary geometry
- remove unseen/internal faces where safe
- use texture detail instead of geometry for microdamage

Each pallet should be ONE practical scene asset when exported, while retaining sensible internal mesh/material organisation in the .blend if useful.

TRANSFORMS / ORIGIN
Before export:
- real-world scale
- Z-up in Blender
- apply transforms
- scale = 1,1,1
- rotation clean
- origin centred horizontally at ground/contact level
- pallet rests exactly on Z=0
- sensible forward orientation
- no accidental floating geometry

GLB EXPORT
Export separately:
- pallet_worn_brown.glb
- pallet_worn_blue.glb

Requirements:
- glTF 2.0 binary (.glb)
- embed required textures
- materials must survive export correctly
- no external texture dependency
- no cameras
- no lights
- no unnecessary collections/objects
- no unsupported shader nodes required at runtime

Also save the editable Blender source:
- pallet_worn_brown.blend
- pallet_worn_blue.blend
or one clearly organised source .blend containing both, if that is substantially cleaner.

VALIDATION
After exporting, REIMPORT each GLB into a clean Blender scene and inspect it.

Verify:
- dimensions
- transforms
- UVs
- normals
- material assignment
- texture embedding
- roughness
- normal strength
- no missing maps
- no unsupported material behaviour
- no obvious z-fighting
- no floating nails
- no texture stretching
- no obvious repeated wood grain
- correct ground placement

If possible, also test the GLBs in a basic glTF/Three.js viewer.

FINAL RENDER / QA
Produce neutral preview renders of each completed pallet:
1. three-quarter upper view
2. lower three-quarter view showing construction/fork openings
3. close-up crop showing texture, edge wear, nails and chipped paint

Use neutral lighting that reveals roughness and surface detail rather than dramatic lighting that conceals problems.

SUCCESS CRITERIA
At first glance:
- one reads unmistakably as an old, raw brown timber pallet
- one reads unmistakably as an old, repeatedly-used BLUE painted pallet

At close range:
- the player sees wood grain, dirt, scratches, nail detail, chipped edges, paint loss and roughness variation rather than a clean CG box with a colour applied.

The BLUE pallet in particular should feel as though it has spent years being handled, stacked, scraped and left outdoors. Its blue paint should tell that history.

Do not stop at “technically correct.” Continue the texture/material pass until neither asset looks plasticky, Lego-like, freshly manufactured or procedurally uniform.
