# Zealot of Harpurhey — Creative, Philosophical and Artistic Handover

This document is not an engineering specification. It is a creative constitution for the world of *Zealot of Harpurhey* and for the wider artistic practice from which the game emerges.

When generating ideas, environments, writing, events, characters, objects, cinematics, sound, clothing, architecture, paintings, photographs, quests or visual interventions, use this document to understand the artist's way of thinking. The objective is not to imitate individual references superficially — it is to understand the system of thought that connects them.

## 1. The game is part of a much larger artistic practice

Zealot of Harpurhey should not be understood as an isolated videogame project. It grows out of a wider practice involving: photography; medium-format photography; filmmaking; documentary; essay film; experimental cinema; painting; drawing; installation; sculpture and objects; clothing; graphic design; writing; philosophy; criticism; archival practice; urban observation; internet culture; advertising; popular culture; mythology; literature; labour; class; contemporary Manchester.

The game is another medium through which these ideas can be staged. It may eventually become the medium that connects almost all of them. A painting can become a wall inside the game. A photograph can become a location. A real photograph of Manchester can become architecture. A T-shirt can become a costume. A short film can become an explorable scene. An essay can become an environmental event. A philosophical proposition can become a game mechanic. A real urban encounter can become a recurring character.

The boundary between the artist's life, photographs, films, writing, clothes and the game should remain porous.

## 2. The spectres are all around us

The most important conceptual framework surrounding the game is: **THE SPECTRES ARE ALL AROUND US**. This is not merely the title of an artwork series — it is a way of looking. It proposes that contemporary reality is saturated with spectres: not necessarily ghosts in the supernatural sense, but a sign, a brand, a memory, an image, a commodity, an ideology, a cultural symbol, an obsolete technology, a piece of language, an architectural convention, a ritual, an advertisement, a political promise, an inherited visual form, a social category, a copy whose original has disappeared, a cultural object whose meaning has survived after its context has been forgotten.

A useful formulation: **a spectre is a piece of meaning whose construction has been forgotten.** The world is full of these things. We encounter them constantly while believing them to be natural. The role of the artist is partly to notice them again.

## 3. The spectre as sign

Semiotics is fundamental. The world should continually ask: What does this object signify? How did it come to signify that? Who decided? What happens when the sign is detached from its original context? What happens when the copy becomes more familiar than what it represents? What happens when a sign remains after the social world that produced it has changed?

A road marking is not merely paint. A delivery bag is not merely a container. A tracksuit is not merely clothing. A bottle is not merely packaging. A bus stop is not merely infrastructure. A streetlight is not merely illumination. A crown is not merely royal decoration. A horse is not merely transport. These are cultural signs. The game should continually find ways of making apparently obvious things become strange enough that the player sees them again.

## 4. Roland Barthes

Especially relevant: the sign; denotation and connotation; myth; cultural codes; photography; the punctum; the transformation of historical constructions into things that appear natural.

Barthes' idea of myth — a historical or ideological construction beginning to appear inevitable or natural — is central. Zealot of Harpurhey can expose these processes through ordinary objects. A food-delivery backpack appears to be a neutral practical object, but it can also signify precarious labour, migration, platform capitalism, urban speed, convenience, invisibility, service work, algorithmic management, contemporary masculinity, metropolitan life.

The game can make such an object monumental: put it under a streetlight, isolate it, repeat it, photograph it, place it inside a museum, have someone carry it on horseback, give it heraldic significance. Suddenly the cultural myth surrounding the object becomes visible. This is a core strategy.

## 5. Baudrillard

Particularly important: simulacra; simulation; copies; hyperreality; signs detached from referents; consumer society; symbolic exchange; objects circulating as signs.

Zealot of Harpurhey is itself already a Baudrillardian object: Manchester reconstructed as code. Real streets become photographs. Photographs become models. Models become GLBs. GLBs become virtual streets. The virtual streets can then be photographed again — those screenshots may become films, those films might return to social media, and social media representations may eventually influence how the real places are remembered. The distinction between the original and the representation becomes unstable. This instability should be embraced.

## 6. The simulacrum is not a failure

Do not assume that a virtual Manchester should simply aspire to perfect correspondence with real Manchester. Its inaccuracies can become meaningful. The virtual street becomes interesting when it occupies the space between memory, photograph, reconstruction, game, dream, cultural image. A copied object can acquire a second existence:

real object → photograph → digital image → game model → painting → 3D-printed object → museum object → merchandise → image again.

At each stage the referent shifts. The work should be interested in that migration.

## 7. Simulacra within simulacra

The game should sometimes deliberately contain representations of representations: a painting based on a photograph hanging inside a virtual reconstruction of the street where the photograph was made; a character wearing a T-shirt containing an artwork based on an object that also exists elsewhere in the game; a poster advertising a fictional exhibition of images taken inside the game; a photograph made in the game appearing later on another wall; a model of a real Manchester shop containing a television displaying footage of its virtual exterior. These recursive structures are particularly appropriate.

## 8. Derrida

Central concepts: deconstruction; différance; trace; absence; presence; supplement; instability of meaning; binaries that depend upon what they exclude; hauntology.

Deconstruction is not "things are broken apart" — it is closer to revealing that supposedly stable meanings depend upon relationships, exclusions and contradictions that destabilise them from within. The game can operate similarly.

## 9. Presence and absence

A city is full of absence. Buildings remain after people leave. Advertising remains after campaigns end. Signs remain after businesses close. Graffiti disappears but leaves stains. A demolished structure survives in photographs. People survive as traces in architecture. The city remembers people without consciously remembering them.

The game should be attentive to empty spaces, missing objects, residue, former uses, demolished places, faded signage, abandoned infrastructure, traces of labour, traces of previous populations. Sometimes what is missing should be more important than what is shown.

## 10. The trace

Every sign contains traces of other signs. Every place contains traces of previous places. Contemporary Ancoats contains industrial Manchester. A fashionable apartment contains the factory. A cycle courier contains the messenger. The delivery rider contains the knight. The mobile phone contains the dispatch office. The app contains the foreman. The supermarket contains the market. The game can reveal these temporal echoes without giving lectures about them.

## 11. Hauntology

The city is haunted — not necessarily by literal ghosts, but by dead industries, obsolete technologies, lost architecture, old advertisements, political promises, previous cultural eras, youth, photographs, old music, previous relationships, class histories, vanished businesses, futures that never arrived. The game should occasionally feel as though multiple periods of Manchester are visible simultaneously. The present is never completely present.

## 12. Deconstruction as game logic

The player may encounter seemingly stable categories that slowly collapse:

- **Work / Quest** — a delivery is work, but videogames call tasks "quests"; the courier gradually becomes a knight.
- **Delivery / Pilgrimage** — travelling between destinations begins as employment and can gradually become pilgrimage.
- **Customer / Lord** — a customer waiting for an item becomes equivalent to a medieval patron.
- **Map / Territory** — the player navigates a representation of Manchester that may eventually begin referring to itself.
- **Player / Character** — the person playing controls a fictionalised labourer, but elements from the artist's own life enter the world; where does autobiography stop and fiction begin?

These unstable binaries are productive.

## 13. City as text

Manchester should be treated as something that can be read: architecture; advertisements; storefronts; road markings; graffiti; clothing; buses; logos; surveillance cameras; construction barriers; pub signs; council notices; menus; discarded packaging. The player is partly a reader. Walking becomes interpretation.

## 14. City as stage

The city is a stage. Urban life continually produces theatrical situations: people stand beneath lights, pedestrians enter and exit architectural frames, bus shelters form miniature sets, shop windows resemble stage flats, crossings determine movement, road markings resemble blocking diagrams, architecture determines entrances and exits. The artist's job is partly to notice these accidental theatres. The game should exaggerate this latent theatricality.

## 15. Streetlights as stage lights

Municipal lighting accidentally produces theatre. A person waiting beneath a lamp becomes a performer. A delivery rider crossing a pool of sodium light becomes choreography. A pedestrian standing alone at a bus stop becomes a tableau. The city is already staging its citizens; the artist only needs to frame them. Central to **Public Illumination Studies** and deeply embedded in Zealot of Harpurhey.

## 16. Public Illumination Studies

An ongoing investigation into artificial urban lighting. Who illuminates the city? Why? For whom? What becomes visible/invisible? What kind of theatre does infrastructure create? What does artificial light do to ordinary bodies?

Subjects: streetlights; bus stops; car parks; underpasses; roads; service areas; municipal architecture; people waiting; workers; delivery riders; empty streets. Public light is simultaneously practical, cinematic, bureaucratic, theatrical, surveillance-like, lonely, beautiful.

## 17. The night

Night is a principal philosophical space of the practice, not merely a time setting. Day explains too much. Night removes information — buildings lose detail, background disappears, light isolates, objects become symbols, distances become uncertain, windows become screens, people become silhouettes, streetlights organise space into discrete islands. Night turns ordinary Manchester into something closer to theatre.

## 18. Night as decontextualisation

At night, objects are partially detached from context — a bench alone, a person surrounded by blackness, a tree as a strange illuminated form, a sign that seems to float, a shelter resembling sculpture. This decontextualisation connects to the semiotic project: the object becomes readable again because its normal context has weakened.

## 19. The unclad night

The night strips the city — removes explanatory detail, leaves structure, light, silhouettes, signs, bodies, architecture. An unclad city means a city temporarily stripped of its daytime ideological costume — but the darkness also conceals. The night is simultaneously revelation and concealment. That contradiction should remain unresolved.

## 20. Photographic origin

The visual world does not originate primarily from game references — it originates from photography. The artist has spent substantial time photographing the real city, especially at night. Many locations, trees, streetlights, objects and architectural situations entering the game come from things actually encountered and photographed. The game is partly an extension of photographic fieldwork.

## 21. Medium-format photography

The artist has used medium-format film cameras (Mamiya RB67/RZ67 family). This encourages deliberate composition, slowness, careful framing, attention to geometry, stillness, significant negative space, considered exposure, waiting, architectural sensitivity. The game should retain some of this mentality — it should not always behave like a fast videogame camera.

## 22. Photographer before game designer

Imagine the artist standing in the virtual street with a medium-format camera. Would this frame be worth photographing? Where would the tripod go? What is the relationship between the figure and the architecture? Where is the pool of light? What happens if the person stands still? This should influence environmental composition.

## 23. Planimetric space

The artist repeatedly gravitates toward frontal framing, flattened architecture, symmetry, orthographic feeling, perpendicular camera relationships, carefully organised depth — evoking tableaux, diagrams, architectural elevations, theatre stages, photographs, surveillance imagery. The game should occasionally allow these compositions to dominate instead of constantly centring the player behind the character.

## 24. The city as photographic laboratory

Manchester is an endless system of experiments. A street corner, a bus stop, a person under a lamp can each become a study — hence titles like *Public Illumination Studies* and *Figures Isolated Within Municipal Architecture*, suggesting pseudo-scientific observation. The artist is both participant and examiner.

## 25. Figures isolated within municipal architecture

A recurring subject: a person rendered small relative to infrastructure — stairwells, public housing, underpasses, concrete walkways, council architecture, car parks, transit infrastructure. The figure becomes both human and diagrammatic; the architecture appears to classify or contain them.

## 26. Municipal architecture

Infrastructure people rarely think of as architecture — pedestrian barriers, traffic islands, bollards, council paving, public stairs, railings, bus shelters, pedestrian bridges, streetlamps, underpasses, CCTV poles — should be treated seriously. A railing can be as important as a cathedral.

## 27. A ballet for municipal architecture

The city already choreographs people: roads tell bodies where to move, railings restrict movement, crossings create rhythm, traffic lights create pauses, escalators move bodies mechanically, turnstiles sequence people, bus doors open and close like stage entrances. The game can occasionally reveal urban movement as choreography. The city is both theatre and choreographer.

## 28. Road markings

Road markings function as legal instruction, diagram, choreography, drawing, notation, measurement. Additional diagrammatic/measurement lines in environments can make the world appear as though someone is studying it — closer to annotations by an obsessive municipal researcher than futuristic HUD decoration.

## 29. Technical drawing

The practice intersects with technical diagrams, measurements, labels, arrows, museum captions, archival categories. This creates tension between irrational subject matter and bureaucratic presentation — an absurd object becomes funnier and stranger when documented with total institutional seriousness.

## 30. The fictional institution

*The Spectres Are All Around Us* can be imagined almost as an institution — a research programme, an archive, a department, a municipal body — that catalogues phenomena and produces photographs, films, diagrams, objects, labels, garments, reports. This allows the entire practice to feel connected.

## 31. Museum logic

Everyday objects (delivery bag, hair product, energy drink, cheap packaging, work equipment, mobile phone, supermarket object) can be removed from use and treated like archaeological artefacts. Once displayed, cast, engraved, painted, classified, photographed, the object changes ontological status. The game can mimic this process.

## 32. Pop noir

Taking ordinary contemporary cultural objects seriously as carriers of memory, identity, ritual, class, masculinity, Black British culture, aspiration, humour, commodity culture — not Pop Art repetition for its own sake, closer to archaeology of contemporary life.

## 33. Paintings

The artist's paintings are not decorative concept art — they belong to the same conceptual universe: interpretations of photographs, distortions of signs, diagrams, spectres, objects, fictional evidence. In the game they can appear in galleries, shops, homes, on billboards, on clothing, inexplicably outdoors, enlarged into architecture.

## 34. Painting as re-encoding

A useful chain: the artist sees something → photographs it → the photograph becomes a painting → the painting becomes a texture → the texture enters the game → the virtual painting is photographed inside the game. Each translation introduces error. That error is useful — it creates another spectre.

## 35. Clothing

Clothing is part of the practice. The game can include garments derived from real works, e.g. a T-shirt reading *"I went to art school and all I got was this lousy T-shirt"* — simultaneously joke, autobiography, criticism, merchandise, institutional commentary. Characters wearing such objects let the artist's physical practice leak into the fictional world.

## 36. TSAU as a brand that critiques branding

The Spectres Are All Around Us can itself take on the appearance of a brand — logos, T-shirts, objects, labels, products. There should remain an unresolved irony: a project investigating commodity signs can itself become a commodity sign. Do not necessarily resolve this contradiction. Use it.

## 37. The delivery bag

One of the most important objects in the game. Should acquire almost heraldic significance — representing work, mobility, platform capitalism, precarious employment, convenience, contemporary urban infrastructure, bodily labour hidden behind apps — while visually resembling armour, a shield, a shell, a burden. This helps create the courier/knight correspondence.

## 38. Gig economy

Philosophical material, not merely game context. The rider appears independent, yet the platform determines jobs, the rating system disciplines behaviour, algorithms allocate work, the worker supplies the vehicle, waiting time and risk are externalised, employment status becomes ambiguous. An excellent contemporary example of unstable categories: worker/contractor, freedom/control, entrepreneur/employee, game/labour.

## 39. Financial precarity

Should remain part of the emotional ground of the project without the protagonist endlessly complaining about money. It can appear structurally: waiting for jobs, calculating whether a delivery is worth travelling for, repairing a bike, choosing whether to buy something, moving constantly for small payments, expensive city spaces surrounding low-paid work, entering luxury environments only as service labour. Money can produce narrative pressure without turning the game into an economy simulator.

## 40. Class

Manchester is highly stratified — luxury development, council architecture, cultural institutions, gig workers, expensive shops, public libraries, decaying infrastructure, nightlife, homelessness, new apartment towers, old industrial areas. Avoid simplistic rich-versus-poor moralising; observe how different forms of value occupy the same streets.

## 41. Labour as invisibility

Much modern labour is designed to disappear — food appears, packages appear, rooms become clean, taxis arrive, content appears online, infrastructure works. The delivery rider makes that labour visible. The game can repeatedly foreground workers who ordinarily remain peripheral to cultural representations of the city.

## 42. The courier as medieval messenger

The delivery rider exists within a much longer history of messengers: herald, messenger, pilgrim, knight, traveller, errand bearer. The app interface becomes the contemporary equivalent of receiving a summons.

## 43. Don Quixote

A major reference, structurally rather than as adaptation. Don Quixote sees the ordinary world through the logic of romance; Zealot of Harpurhey can similarly allow contemporary Manchester to be read through chivalry, myth, literature, philosophy. A delivery rider can become a knight without the physical world changing very much — the transformation happens through interpretation.

## 44. Knighthood

The player being knighted should be simultaneously ridiculous, moving, sincere, theatrical — referencing Don Quixote persuading an innkeeper to knight him. A mundane service interaction becomes medieval ritual.

## 45. The horse

One of the strongest possible surreal objects. A contemporary delivery rider travelling through Manchester on horseback collapses historical periods. The horse should be treated completely seriously — no wink to camera. It creates: courier / rider / knight / gig worker / medieval messenger.

## 46. Mythology

Recurring myths and literary structures: Icarus; knight errantry; Hamlet and Ophelia; classical tragedy; Christian imagery; quests; ascent and fall; pilgrimage. These should not appear as costume references — contemporary life should be allowed to reproduce ancient structures.

## 47. Icarus

Connects ambition, ascent, technology, youth, hubris, beauty, failure, falling. In a contemporary city: tower blocks, elevators, cranes, aircraft, dreams of success, artistic ambition, economic mobility, falling from status. Adaptable rather than illustrated literally.

## 48. The fall

Falling is simultaneously physical, moral, emotional, social, economic, mythological. Vertical architecture becomes symbolically charged: stairs, balconies, towers, bridges, roofs, lifts.

## 49. Hamlet and Ophelia

Provides another literary constellation around love, distrust, performance, surveillance, madness, death, water, language. Avoid direct adaptation unless deliberately staged — the important question is how these structures might reappear inside contemporary relationships.

## 50. Cervantes' Curious Impertinent

Its obsession with testing love until the test destroys the relationship connects to paranoia, proof, observation, surveillance, desire for certainty — useful in a practice already concerned with images and evidence.

## 51. Rilke

Rilke's First Elegy has already been used in the artist's moving-image practice. The line between beauty and terror in Rilke is deeply relevant — the city at night can be beautiful, alienating, sublime, dangerous, tender. Poetry can operate within the game as atmosphere rather than explanation.

## 52. The broken-down car / Rilke scene

A film scene (broken-down car, night, a woman, the First Elegy, quiet urban space) is intended eventually to exist inside the game — a crucial model for how previous films can become world fragments. The player might encounter the scene almost as though they have entered an existing film.

## 53. Life on Earth

Small cinematic vignettes where ordinary locations become philosophically charged — a bus stop can carry Rilke, a road can carry Purcell, a mundane Manchester setting can briefly become metaphysical. Very appropriate for the game.

## 54. Night Movements

The Night Movement series establishes night as a recurring compositional/conceptual space (e.g. *Night Movement No. I*), involving lovers, mathematics, failed attempts to represent emotion, architecture, isolated figures, poetry. "Movement" suggests music, bodies, transport, cinematic sequence, emotional movement. The game can contain Night Movements as episodic events.

## 55. Mathematics versus the sublime

A recurring strategy: attempting to describe mathematically things that resist mathematical description — love, grief, beauty, the sublime. This tension between measurement/diagram/formula and emotion/transcendence/impossibility is important. A formula written onto a romantic image stages the failure of rational representation.

## 56. "The sublime cannot be represented mathematically"

Captures the tension between bureaucracy/measurement/engineering/administration and beauty/terror/desire/transcendence. The city is measured constantly (land, roads, bodies, economic value), yet much of what makes life meaningful resists measurement.

## 57. The archive

The artist's background in digital archiving intersects with the creative practice. An archive appears objective but depends on selection, metadata, naming, omission, categorisation, institutional power. The game may increasingly behave like an archive — objects collected, events recorded, photographs stored, places reconstructed — yet fictional and subjective. This tension is productive.

## 58. The game as archive

The game may become an archive of Manchester, personal memories, photographs, films, artworks, friends, architecture, shops, cultural moments. Some places may disappear in reality while surviving inside the game.

## 59. Manchester

Not generic urban scenery. Carries industrial history, post-industrial transformation, music, rave culture, football, migration, Black British culture, redevelopment, municipal history, radical politics, universities, warehouses, canals, rain, brick, glass towers, social housing. These layers should coexist.

## 60. Industrial spectres

Manchester's industrial past is one of its strongest hauntological layers — warehouses become apartments, factories become restaurants, canals become leisure spaces, industrial infrastructure becomes aesthetic heritage. The labour disappears while the architecture remains. Almost a perfect TSAU phenomenon.

## 61. Ancoats

Historical Manchester and contemporary redevelopment physically overlap, allowing temporal bleeding — a fashionable street momentarily revealing workers, machinery, smoke, earlier street geometry, not as literal ghosts but as visual traces.

## 62. Castlefield

Canals, viaducts, brick, bridges, water, rail infrastructure, darkness, reflections — a region of solitude, navigation, uncertainty, industrial memory.

## 63. Central Library

Can function as a civic temple — a modern Temple of Knowledge. Books inside might contain descriptions of the player's actions, introducing metafiction, archive, destiny, textual self-reference.

## 64. John Rylands

Offers Gothic potential — corridors that might not follow ordinary geometry; literary figures appearing without explanation; architecture becoming memory.

## 65. Chetham's

Can imply that the courier's role predates delivery applications — messengers have always moved through the city; the technology changes, the structure persists.

## 66. Manchester Art Gallery

Creates a direct bridge between the real artist and the fictional world — the artist's own paintings and photographs might appear within the virtual gallery, so the game exhibits the practice that created it. Highly appropriate recursion.

## 67. The Portico and other libraries

Represent classification, memory, authority, knowledge, texts, institutional preservation — aligning with the archival dimension of TSAU.

## 68. Bus stops

Small public stages combining waiting, advertising, shelter, light, movement, class, public transport. People repeatedly occupy them temporarily — almost perfect minimalist theatres.

## 69. Waiting

Philosophically important. Gig workers wait, passengers wait, lovers wait, people wait for buses, for money, for messages. Modern capitalism presents itself as speed, while enormous amounts of life are spent waiting. The game should not fear occasional slowness.

## 70. The queue

Another urban choreography — people arrange themselves automatically according to social convention. The type of ordinary phenomenon the practice should notice.

## 71. Surveillance

The contemporary city is constantly watching: CCTV, phones, doorbell cameras, dashcams, body cameras, social media, the artist's camera, the player's camera. Surveillance sits beside photography. Who is allowed to look? What changes when a person knows they are being observed?

## 72. Photography and surveillance

The camera is morally ambiguous — it can preserve, aestheticise, document, exploit, surveil. The game does not need to resolve this. The in-game camera can eventually make the player aware that photographing something also transforms it into an image-object.

## 73. Player as photographer

The photography mechanic should have philosophical significance — the player converts world into image, not simply collects. Those images could later appear inside the game, become posters, become archival documents, influence events, become evidence.

## 74. The world after photography

Once photographed, an event gains another life. Perhaps an event disappears after being photographed; perhaps the photograph becomes more persistent than the event itself. A mechanic worth fitting the project.

## 75. Realism and artificiality

Deliberately situated between hyper-real, game-like, photographic, uncanny. Not perfect photorealism — that risks making the world invisible again. A slight artificiality allows the player to notice it.

## 76. Uncanny realism

The world should frequently feel recognisable but not completely secure. Sources of uncanniness: static people, repetition, unexpected scale, temporal discontinuity, impossible appearances, things treated as normal that clearly are not, overly theatrical lighting, architecture becoming too symmetrical, paintings appearing physically in the world, old and new periods overlapping. Avoid horror clichés unless specifically relevant.

## 77. Deadpan surrealism

The world should rarely announce that something surreal is happening. The horse rider simply passes. The Queen simply waits. A classical painting simply appears near a bus stop. Nobody screams. This deadpan tone is essential.

## 78. The everyday must remain

Do not allow mythology to erase everyday Manchester. A person buys an energy drink, a rider checks their phone, someone waits for a bus, a shop closes, rain falls — then something impossible happens. The mundane provides the necessary ground.

## 79. Myth inside the mundane

Ancient structures continue operating inside contemporary life: the knight survives as courier, the pilgrimage survives as commute, the oracle survives as algorithm, the herald survives as notification, the castle survives as tower block, the marketplace survives as platform. The myth is not gone — its costume changed.

## 80. Algorithm as oracle

The delivery app appears rational but to the worker can behave mysteriously — why did this job appear, why has work stopped, why was another rider selected. The algorithm becomes almost divine: invisible, opaque, powerful. It dispenses tasks; the rider obeys.

## 81. Digital feudalism

A productive tension between modern platform labour and medieval social structures — the worker appears technologically advanced but can remain dependent on distant powers. Reinforces the knight imagery without becoming literal political allegory.

## 82. Heroism and absurdity

The project should remain uncertain about heroism. Delivery work genuinely requires endurance, navigation, patience, risk, physical effort — yet framing every delivery as heroic is absurd. That tension is useful.

## 83. Dignity

Do not treat gig workers merely as symbols of exploitation. They have agency, humour, style, friendships, skills, ambitions, interior lives. Avoid patronising representations of labour.

## 84. Black British cultural life

The broader practice emerges from contemporary Black British life and Manchester cultural experience — music, fashion, hair products, shops, speech, objects, food, nightlife, social spaces, art. Avoid turning these details into anthropological spectacle; they are part of ordinary lived reality.

## 85. Everyday objects as cultural archives

Hair products, drinks, trainers, bags, clothes, packaging can contain enormous social history. The practice should remain attentive to them.

## 86. Objects have biographies

Production → shop → ownership → use → damage → disposal → photograph → memory → archive. The game can sometimes reveal these biographies.

## 87. Soft Surveillance / Soft Machine

Existing TSAU object titles (*Soft Surveillance*, *Soft Machine*, *Rectangle with Embellishments*, *Home Improvement*, *Spectres Under Glass*, *Two Friends Share a Walk at Night*, *Side Bag with Embellishments*) illustrate a naming strategy: dry, classificatory, slightly bureaucratic, slightly poetic. Reusable.

## 88. Titling

Titles should often make ordinary scenes feel like museum objects or research specimens. Avoid overdramatic fantasy names. Good logic: *Figure Beneath Public Illumination No. III*, *Temporary Monument for a Delivery Rider*, *Two People Waiting for the Last Bus*, *Object Intended to Carry Dinner*.

## 89. Humour through classification

Calling an obvious thing something excessively analytical can create humour — e.g. a delivery bag becomes *Mobile Insulated Food Transportation Device with Reflective Embellishments*. Use sparingly.

## 90. The game as taxonomy

The world may eventually classify lights, benches, riders, objects, gestures, architectures, signs. A taxonomy can begin orderly and gradually become absurd.

## 91. Philosophy should be embodied

Avoid NPCs reciting philosophical lectures. Instead: Baudrillard becomes duplicated shops; Derrida becomes traces and unstable meanings; Barthes becomes mythologised everyday objects; Benjamin becomes wandering through the city; Debord becomes spectacle; Rilke becomes a voice beside a broken-down car; Cervantes becomes a courier riding a horse. Theory should become environment.

## 92. Walter Benjamin

Relevant: the flâneur; fragments; urban wandering; commodity culture; arcades; history; montage; collecting. The player is partly a contemporary flâneur — but unlike the classical flâneur, the courier is working and cannot simply wander without consequence. This tension is important.

## 93. The working flâneur

Forced to observe the city while moving through it for economic reasons, the rider sees back streets, service entrances, apartment lobbies, restaurants, alleys, expensive buildings, private thresholds. The labourer becomes an accidental urban ethnographer.

## 94. Debord and the spectacle

The contemporary city is saturated by images — advertising, brands, screens, social media. Architecture becomes branding; neighbourhoods become lifestyle products. The game can occasionally expose that spectacular layer.

## 95. Dérive

Although deliveries give destinations, the player should retain opportunities to drift. Some of the most meaningful discoveries may happen between objectives. The world should reward curiosity.

## 96. Psychogeography

Different locations should not simply look different — they can create different emotional and philosophical states. Architecture affects behaviour; light affects attention; a neighbourhood has psychological force. More interesting than building a large map purely for scale.

## 97. Districts as ideas

Each new area should ideally introduce a conceptual idea: Central Library = knowledge/archive/fate; Castlefield = infrastructure/solitude/industrial memory; Ancoats = history overwritten by redevelopment; Harpurhey = origin/class/local mythology; Art Gallery = representation/institution/recursive authorship. Expansion should deepen the thesis.

## 98. The North of England

Belongs specifically to northern England — not aesthetically interchangeable with London or a generic global city. Northern weather, humour, architecture, public infrastructure and cultural memory matter.

## 99. Rain

Not merely atmospheric. Rain changes surfaces, reflects signs, extends streetlight, empties streets, changes sound, alters movement. Wet asphalt can turn municipal light into cinema.

## 100. Trees

Real trees photographed by the artist are being reconstructed as game assets — particularly hanging branches, unusual silhouettes, isolated illuminated foliage, trees caught dramatically by streetlights. A tree at night becomes almost sculptural.

## 101. Nature within municipal space

Trees growing beside roads and estates create a productive contradiction: organic growth inside heavily regulated urban geometry. Can be visually beautiful.

## 102. Streetlight as sun

At night, municipal infrastructure replaces celestial illumination — the streetlight becomes an artificial sun, a privately manufactured or council-installed object determining what can be seen. Philosophically rich.

## 103. Windows

At night become screens — reveal fragments, and let the viewer see another person's life as image. Connects cinema, voyeurism and the city.

## 104. Shop windows

Turn commodities into theatre — staged, lighted, separated by glass, presented for desire. The shop window is already an exhibition.

## 105. Glass

Produces transparency, reflection, separation, surveillance, display, screens. Use thoughtfully.

## 106. Reflection

Creates duplicated worlds — water, windows, wet roads, mirrors. A reflection is a natural simulacrum.

## 107. Screens

Screens inside screens are especially appropriate — phones, shop monitors, advertisements, CCTV, computers, cinema. The entire game itself is viewed through a screen. Use this recursion.

## 108. The player's computer as final frame

The player is ultimately looking at Manchester through a screen. The game itself participates in the same representational problem it investigates. Do not forget this.

## 109. Fourth-wall instability

Metafiction is appropriate when subtle — a book describing the player's actions, a poster depicting the player's previous photograph, an NPC referring to something outside ordinary chronology. Avoid constant fourth-wall jokes; they should feel unsettling, not comedic.

## 110. Time

Time does not need to behave entirely realistically. Certain moments may persist; some scenes may repeat; some characters may appear trapped inside tableaux; a film scene may eternally replay. Can suggest memory.

## 111. Memory

The virtual city can be understood partly as memory rather than simulation — memory compresses, exaggerates, omits, combines, repeats. Geographical inaccuracies can feel psychologically appropriate.

## 112. Autobiography

The project can incorporate the artist's life without becoming straightforward autobiography — friends, jobs, objects, places, photographs, conversations, experiences fictionalised. Translates a real personal archive into myth.

## 113. The artist as world-builder

Photography frames a piece of the city; painting reframes the photograph; the game now allows the artist to reconstruct the entire frame. A significant development in the practice.

## 114. Game as synthesis

The game has the potential to synthesise previously separate parts of the practice — photography, painting, writing, theory, film, clothes, music, sculpture, archives, performance — becoming a Gesamtkunstwerk-like container that retains the texture of Manchester rather than becoming grandiose.

## 115. Future short films

A critical long-term function of the game is as a filmmaking environment — placing characters, selecting locations, composing cameras, scripting events, recording scenes, creating short films from the virtual Manchester. Every strong location is also potentially a set.

## 116. Machinima as artistic practice

Screen-recording the game should not be treated as secondary promotional content — it can become original moving-image work. The virtual city can become a studio.

## 117. Film within game / game within film

Real film footage may enter the game; the game may generate film; the generated film may return to gallery exhibition. Boundaries should remain deliberately unstable.

## 118. Social media

Short episodic pieces for Instagram/TikTok/other platforms could circulate as autonomous artworks without necessarily explaining they come from a game.

## 119. Characters as performers

NPCs can function almost like actors in a repertory company — the same model appearing in different roles. Intentional theatricality.

## 120. Greek chorus

Groups of characters can operate like a chorus — observing, repeating, commenting, moving together. Bridges classical theatre and the contemporary street.

## 121. Music

Should be capable of shifting a mundane location into another emotional register. Classical music against ordinary urban space can be especially powerful when used sincerely rather than ironically (Purcell has already appeared in the wider cinematic language).

## 122. Silence

Equally important — removing city sound can suddenly make a familiar location metaphysical. Do not fill every moment with music.

## 123. Poetry

Can enter the world as spoken voice, text, graffiti, subtitles, environmental writing, recorded performance — not necessarily lore exposition. Should be spatial: where something is spoken matters.

## 124. Poetic justice

The artist's poetry-centred filmmaking has presented poets inside carefully controlled night tableaux, reinforcing that the city itself can become a stage for language.

## 125. Hard light

A single hard artificial light isolates, creates a visible stage, can make a person appear almost cut out from the environment. The game can use similar lighting structures.

## 126. Darkness as negative space

Do not fear blackness. Photography depends on negative space; the game can too. Not every surface requires illumination.

## 127. Philosophical contradictions to preserve

Real/fake, work/play, labour/quest, ordinary/mythological, photograph/simulation, archive/fiction, freedom/algorithmic control, commodity/artefact, street/stage, worker/knight, brand/critique of branding, documentation/surveillance, memory/reconstruction, beauty/precarity, night/illumination, visibility/concealment, original/copy, Manchester/virtual Manchester. Do not rush to resolve these — the contradiction is often the artwork.

## 128. Beauty and precarity

The game should present a beautifully lit city while acknowledging the person moving through it may be financially precarious. Aesthetic beauty should not erase economic reality; economic critique should not require visual misery.

## 129. Art and work

The artist occupies overlapping positions: photographer, filmmaker, artist, archivist, worker, freelancer. The game's courier similarly crosses categories — an autobiographical structural resonance worth preserving.

## 130. Don't over-explain

Trust the image. A horse rider passing through Manchester does not require explanatory dialogue. A painting appearing beside a bus stop does not require lore. A person reciting Rilke beside a broken-down car does not require a quest marker. The player can encounter something without mastering its meaning.

## 131. The player should sometimes be confused

Ontological confusion is productive: Did that actually happen? Was that person there before? Why is this painting outdoors? Why does this book mention me? This uncertainty suits the project.

## 132. But the game should not become random

Surrealism must remain motivated. Every strange event should connect to philosophy, autobiography, Manchester, mythology, labour, photography, art history, memory. Random weirdness weakens the project.

## 133. An important test

When proposing a surreal element, ask: why does this belong specifically to Daniel's world? If the answer could just as easily describe any indie surreal game, reconsider it.

## 134. Art-historical cutaways

Historical paintings may occasionally intrude into contemporary Manchester. The collision between a canonical image and an ordinary northern street creates meaning — a painting can haunt the world like cultural memory.

## 135. Photoshop as memory machine

Some of the artist's Photoshop experiments can become spatial events — digital collage as impossible spatial construction, made inhabitable in the game.

## 136. Unreal physical combinations

Objects can occasionally coexist in ways possible only within the virtual world — but these combinations should usually be visually calm. The more impossible the concept, the more deadpan the presentation.

## 137. Scale

Changing scale can create symbolic emphasis — a delivery bag becoming monumental, a small product becoming architecture, a road marking extending impossibly. Use very selectively.

## 138. Repetition

Repeated identical objects or people can expose simulation — a repeated sign becomes uncanny, a duplicated person becomes simulacral. Repetition should have conceptual purpose.

## 139. Error

Digital error can occasionally become aesthetic material — clipping, texture errors, loading, LOD transitions — but avoid fetishising glitches generically. A technical glitch is not automatically meaningful.

## 140. Old game aesthetics

Earlier development explored Dreamcast/PS2-like aesthetics. Should remain as residue rather than dominant style — visible geometry, old UI behaviour, slightly artificial characters, visible simulation — but the current direction is toward a richer space between photographic reality and game construction.

## 141. The uncanny gap

The most interesting visual space: too real to read as deliberately retro, yet too artificial to disappear into realism. That slight mismatch invites attention.

## 142. Player speed

Slower traversal can support observation. The project should not always reward maximal speed. Cycling can create rhythm; walking should reveal detail; stopping should occasionally matter.

## 143. Commuting

A ritual — repeated routes transform geography into memory. The game can allow familiar routes to gradually change.

## 144. Repetition and difference

A repeated delivery route becomes philosophically interesting with small variations: same street, different light, different person, different event. Resonates with Deleuzian repetition and difference without requiring explicit citation.

## 145. Deleuze and Guattari

Useful around assemblage, networks, flows, deterritorialisation, reterritorialisation, rhizomes. A city is an assemblage of people, roads, capital, data, vehicles, signs, buildings, desire. The delivery rider moves through those flows.

## 146. Assemblage

A delivery is produced by an enormous invisible assemblage — restaurant, app, server, rider, road, phone, GPS, customer, payment system, building. The apparently simple act contains the whole city.

## 147. Logistics

Aesthetically invisible but socially fundamental — warehouses, routes, dispatch, storage, delivery. The game can make logistical systems visible.

## 148. GPS

Turns the city into an abstract diagram. The rider simultaneously experiences physical Manchester and map Manchester. This map/territory tension is conceptually valuable.

## 149. Maps

Already interpretations — a map decides what matters and is never neutral. The game's map can occasionally reveal this.

## 150. Lostness

Sometimes not knowing exactly where you are is useful. Urban wandering should retain mystery — navigation should not be completely frictionless.

## 151. Pilgrimage

A longer journey across the virtual city can acquire ritual significance. A delivery route may unexpectedly become pilgrimage — the transformation should emerge slowly.

## 152. Religious language

"Zealot" in the title introduces religious intensity, not necessarily literal — zeal can mean obsessive belief, artistic conviction, devotion, ideology, work, quest. The title should remain slightly mysterious.

## 153. Harpurhey

Gives the title geographical specificity and class resonance, preventing the project from becoming abstract fantasy. A zealot belongs somewhere — the local and the mythological collide.

## 154. The knight without armour

The courier is perhaps an unclad knight — stripped of horse, castle, armour, aristocratic title, yet the structural role persists: they travel, carry things, receive missions, encounter strangers, endure weather. A powerful contemporary myth.

## 155. Armour as workwear

Modern protective clothing can function symbolically as armour — helmet, reflective jacket, delivery bag, gloves, bike. The visual analogy should remain subtle.

## 156. The phone as sword / shield / oracle

The smartphone provides directions, orders, payment, communication, ratings — without it the courier cannot work. Treat it as infrastructure, not merely UI.

## 157. Capitalism as environment

Capitalism does not need to appear as a villain — it appears as the structure of the world: prices, jobs, rent, advertisements, opening hours, property, brands, labour. The player inhabits these systems.

## 158. Advertising

Should be considered semiotically — what does the city promise, what bodies appear, what lifestyle is imagined, what does a billboard look like at 2am when nobody is there? At night, advertising can become strangely theological.

## 159. Brand spectres

Familiar logos can function almost like modern heraldry — instantly recognisable, organising expectations. Brand marks make excellent contemporary spectres.

## 160. Heraldry

Medieval heraldry and contemporary branding have interesting structural similarities — both create instantly recognisable symbolic systems. A delivery-company logo on a bag can function like a coat of arms.

## 161. Flags

Potent signs — nation, football, territory, identity, protest. A flag under artificial light can become a powerful isolated image.

## 162. England

The artist has photographed and filmed England flags as part of nocturnal urban imagery. The flag should remain ambiguous — patriotism, football, territory, nationalism, domestic decoration — meaning depends on context.

## 163. Cultural ambiguity

Do not flatten ambiguous signs into one political reading. The practice is strongest when the sign remains available for interpretation.

## 164. Architectural framing

Doorways, windows, underpasses, stairwells, bridges produce frames within frames. The built city behaves like a camera.

## 165. The city photographs people

Perhaps the city itself is a photographic apparatus — streetlights expose people, CCTV records them, architecture frames them, screens display them. The artist merely notices.

## 166. Light as classification

A person becomes important simply because they are illuminated. The streetlight selects. This can feel almost religious.

## 167. Halo

A practical lamp can accidentally become a halo, connecting religious imagery to municipal infrastructure. No explanation required.

## 168. The sacred and the mundane

Sacred visual structures should repeatedly surround ordinary subjects — a courier beneath a lamp becomes saint-like, a supermarket object becomes relic, a library becomes temple, a broken car becomes altar.

## 169. Relics

Contemporary objects can function like relics once separated from normal use — connects directly to TSAU objects and museum presentation.

## 170. Shrines

Small urban arrangements sometimes resemble shrines — flowers at a roadside, candles, photographs, football scarves, graffiti. These deserve attention.

## 171. Death

Should not dominate the game, but mortality naturally enters through memorials, lost places, vanished people, photography, archive, night, Rilke. Photography always contains a temporal wound: the photographed moment is already gone.

## 172. Camera Lucida

Barthes: the photograph says "this has been." A reconstructed game version says something stranger: "this may have been, and now exists again differently."

## 173. Digital resurrection

Rebuilding a demolished or changed place in the game can resemble resurrection — but it is not restoration, it is simulation. The resurrected place becomes another spectre.

## 174. Future ruins

The game might eventually preserve places that have not yet disappeared. One day the virtual copy could outlive the real shop.

## 175. Artistic fieldwork

Real-world photography should continue feeding the game — the artist goes outside, observes, photographs, returns, reconstructs, then re-enters the reconstructed city. This loop is itself part of the project.

## 176. The game should send the artist back outside

The virtual world should not replace real-world observation — it should generate new questions (what else should be photographed? what tree? what sign? what strange municipal structure?), creating a feedback loop between street and simulation.

## 177. No totalising theory

Do not attempt to make every scene prove every concept. The world needs air — some things can simply be beautiful, funny, or exist.

## 178. Theory as pressure, not explanation

Theory should operate like gravity — influencing everything without always being visible.

## 179. Aesthetic seriousness without pomposity

The practice can discuss Derrida, Barthes, Baudrillard, Rilke, Benjamin, Cervantes while also caring about a delivery bag, a tracksuit, a broken bike, a bottle, a bus stop, a bad sign. That equality is central — do not make philosophy an excuse to abandon everyday life.

## 180. High and low culture

Shakespeare beside delivery apps, Rilke beside a broken car, Purcell beside a bus stop, classical painting beside Photoshop, fine art beside merchandise. These collisions should not be ironic by default — they should suggest that all these systems genuinely coexist.

## 181. Do not apologise for intellectual range

The world can contain philosophy, fashion, rap, classical music, medium-format photography, computer graphics and urban infrastructure simultaneously. The synthesis is the practice.

## 182. The world is already surreal

Do not always invent surrealism — contemporary reality already contains absurd combinations (a Deliveroo rider beneath a Victorian monument, a 19th-century warehouse containing luxury flats, an ancient church beside LED advertising, a person watching TikTok inside a Gothic library). The artist's role is often simply to frame reality correctly.

## 183. Observation before invention

Before creating something impossible, ask whether the real city already contains something equally strange.

## 184. Art direction as argument

Every environment should make an argument — not a literal statement, an argument through relations. A tiny rider beneath a huge illuminated tower argues something about labour and scale, without dialogue.

## 185. Composition is philosophy

Where a character stands, how large they are in frame, what surrounds them — these decisions communicate theoretical relationships more effectively than exposition.

## 186. Scale of human to city

The person is often small, the architecture often large — producing vulnerability, anonymity, awe, alienation, sublime feeling.

## 187. But people are not insignificant

Even when small in frame, the person remains the emotional centre. Do not let architecture become sterile.

## 188. Loneliness

Night photography often produces solitude — a single figure beneath light can feel profoundly alone. Available in the game without making every scene depressing.

## 189. Intimacy

Small gestures between people can become extremely intimate against the scale of the city — two friends walking at night is enough.

## 190. Two friends share a walk at night

Nothing extraordinary needs to happen. Ordinary human presence can itself be worthy of study.

## 191. Sociality

The night is not only lonely — it includes nightlife, friendship, smoking outside venues, taxis, workers, lovers, crowds. Do not reduce night to empty streets.

## 192. Night workers

Pay attention to people whose working day happens at night — drivers, cleaners, security, bar staff, delivery riders, transport workers. Their city is different from daytime Manchester.

## 193. Public / private

Night destabilises public and private space — shop interiors glow outward, windows expose homes, cars become temporary rooms, the street becomes intimate.

## 194. The car

Cars function as small mobile interiors. A broken-down car especially creates an interruption in movement — the machine stops, time changes (explains the Rilke scene's emotional charge).

## 195. Machines

Should be understood culturally — bike, car, phone, camera, streetlight, CCTV each extend the body and change perception.

## 196. Human / machine

The delivery rider is partly integrated into a technological system — phone + bicycle + navigation + platform + body. The worker becomes an assemblage.

## 197. Soft machine

The body itself may be the soft component inside urban logistics.

## 198. The spectre is not always negative

Spectres can be beautiful, funny, loving, nostalgic, frightening, political. Do not interpret spectrality only as horror or decay.

## 199. Love

Failed love, misunderstanding, proof, mathematics, longing, absence, night — can appear through characters without turning the project into romance gameplay.

## 200. Miscommunication

Signs fail, language fails, images fail, people misunderstand each other — philosophically compatible with the instability of meaning.

## 201. Language

Words themselves can become objects — captions, signs, subtitles, equations, labels. The graphic treatment of language is part of the visual practice.

## 202. Subtitles

Can sometimes remain visible even when speech disappears — text as spectral residue.

## 203. Names

Naming objects changes them. An ordinary object given a title becomes artwork — a Duchampian mechanism quietly behind much of TSAU.

## 204. Duchamp

The readymade demonstrates that context can transform an object's status. TSAU repeatedly performs similar acts.

## 205. But TSAU is not simply readymades

Adds photography, semiotics, Black British cultural context, municipal infrastructure, Manchester, night, fictional taxonomy — making it distinct.

## 206. Kosuth / conceptual art

The relationship between object, image, word is important — a thing can exist simultaneously as physical object, photograph and description. The game can literalise this.

## 207. Magritte

The gap between image and object is relevant — a virtual delivery bag is not a delivery bag, yet the player interacts with it as one. This semiotic slippage is intrinsic to the medium.

## 208. The game medium is philosophically ideal

Videogames are built from representations that pretend to be functional objects — a door is code behaving as door, money is a number behaving as value, a human is a mesh behaving as person. Videogames are inherently semiotic machines. The game should exploit this.

## 209. Rules as meaning

A mechanic can itself make a philosophical argument — e.g. if the player can photograph something only once before it disappears, that mechanic says something about memory. Mechanics should occasionally operate at this level.

## 210. Labour as mechanic

The repetitive act of delivery can itself communicate labour. The question is whether repetition becomes meaningful rather than tedious.

## 211. Interruption

Surreal events become more powerful when they interrupt routine — delivery, delivery, delivery, then a horse, then normality resumes. Rhythm matters.

## 212. Pacing

Do not overload the player with symbolic events. Meaning requires intervals — the mundane sections make the strange sections stronger.

## 213. Rarity

The most surreal events should often be relatively rare. The player should wonder whether they really saw them.

## 214. Local legends

Over time the game could develop its own legends — players telling each other "there is a rider on a horse near Piccadilly after midnight." Discoverable mythology is ideal.

## 215. The city as labyrinth

Occasionally labyrinthine, but unlike fantasy labyrinths the material remains ordinary — streets, underpasses, libraries, housing estates.

## 216. Bureaucracy

Can itself become surreal — forms, numbers, applications, receipts, delivery codes, archive metadata, institutional language. This dryness pairs well with absurd events.

## 217. Numbers

Can become visual motifs — order numbers, building numbers, timestamps, coordinates, measurements, prices. The quantification of life is an important contemporary condition.

## 218. Economic value vs cultural value

A painting may be economically valuable, a delivery may pay £4, a discarded object may have enormous emotional value. The game can juxtapose different systems of valuation.

## 219. Art institutions

Museums and galleries determine what receives cultural value. TSAU's museum-like presentation of everyday objects should remain partly a critique of these systems.

## 220. The game itself may enter a gallery

The game may eventually exist as both a website game and a gallery work. Design the creative identity so it can survive both contexts.

## 221. Playing in a gallery

A viewer might play the game inside an exhibition containing physical paintings or objects from the same world, the virtual and physical versions referring to each other. An extremely promising long-term structure.

## 222. Clothing as portal between worlds

If a shirt exists both in-game and physically, the person wearing the real shirt becomes connected to the fictional world — a powerful extension of the simulacrum.

## 223. Merchandise as artwork

Do not automatically separate "merch" from "art." The commercial form itself can become conceptual material.

## 224. Commodity critique that also sells things

This contradiction is worth embracing rather than hiding — the work critiques commodity culture while producing commodities. Contemporary and honest.

## 225. Authorship

The world should remain clearly authored. Do not smooth away eccentric details for broad commercial legibility — the idiosyncrasy is the value.

## 226. What Claude should do with this document

When asked to generate creative ideas, first identify which threads from this constitution are relevant. Do not dump every concept into every scene. Ask: What is this scene about? Which conceptual tension belongs here? What could remain implicit? What image would communicate the idea?

## 227. Example creative reasoning

If asked to design a delivery mission, do not begin "the player delivers a package and gets £10." Instead consider what the delivery reveals about labour, the city, representation, myth, memory. E.g.: the player delivers flowers to a person living above Vinyl Exchange; the journey is mundane; on leaving, every record-store poster now depicts the player's journey; no explanation; the delivery itself remains normal; the spectre appears afterwards.

## 228. Example environmental reasoning

If asked to design a bus stop scene, consider municipal lighting, waiting, glass, advertising, isolation, public transport, theatre. Perhaps one person stands exactly within the pool of light; behind them an advertisement contains an old master painting; the road is empty; the bus never arrives; no exposition necessary.

## 229. Example photography event

A player photographs a particular illuminated tree. Later the photograph appears framed inside a shop. Eventually the tree itself disappears. The photograph remains. Combines Barthes, archive, memory, simulacra, photography.

## 230. Example gig-economy / myth event

The player accepts what appears to be a standard delivery. At the destination waits a formally dressed figure holding a ceremonial sword who knights the player. The app immediately sends another £4.80 delivery. Captures the tonal world exceptionally well.

## 231. Example Derridian event

A shop sign has been removed. Only the cleaner rectangle of brick remains. The missing sign is more visible than the sign ever was — a trace, without needing to call it one.

## 232. Example Baudrillard event

The player encounters a virtual advertisement showing a photograph of the same virtual street. Inside that photographed street is the same advertisement. The recursion continues just far enough to become strange.

## 233. Example Benjamin event

A normal delivery route sends the player through a covered shopping passage. For several minutes the delivery objective disappears — the player simply wanders among commodities and reflections. The task eventually returns. The forced worker temporarily becomes flâneur.

## 234. Example TSAU object

A delivery bag displayed behind glass in Manchester Art Gallery. Caption: *"Insulated Courier Vessel, Early Platform Period. Synthetic textile, reflective polymer, food residue. Artist unknown."* Exact language can vary; this institutional deadpan is highly appropriate.

## 235. What to avoid

Generic liminal-space clichés; endless fluorescent corridors; VHS horror; random mannequins; generic "backrooms"; unexplained creepy smiles; spooky glitches; cyberpunk neon; dream sequences that mean nothing; lore dumps; fake-deep quotations; philosophy NPCs; excessive magical particles. The project should remain grounded in observed life.

## 236. Don't make everything symbolic

A chip shop can simply be a chip shop. Symbolism gains force when it emerges unexpectedly.

## 237. Don't make everything miserable

Precarity is real, but the world should also contain humour, friendship, music, beautiful light, absurdity, pleasure, style.

## 238. Don't make everything pretty

Retain grime, awkwardness, bad signage, ugly architecture, cheap materials. Manchester should not become a luxury architectural render.

## 239. Don't make everyone a philosopher

Most characters should speak like people. The philosophy should be structural.

## 240. Don't mistake complexity for depth

One strong image can contain more philosophy than ten paragraphs of dialogue.

## 241. Central creative method

Observe something ordinary → isolate it → reframe it → classify it → connect it to another historical or philosophical system → return it to the world slightly altered. One of the central engines of TSAU.

## 242. Central visual method

Observe the city at night. Wait. Find a pool of artificial light. Place or discover a figure within it. Allow surrounding darkness to remove unnecessary information. Frame carefully.

## 243. Central philosophical method

Question things that appear natural. Who constructed this meaning? What history made it possible? What has been excluded? What remains as trace? What happens if the sign moves context? What happens if the copy replaces the original?

## 244. Central narrative method

Begin mundane. Allow myth or philosophy to emerge gradually. Return to mundane life.

## 245. Central tonal method

Sincere + absurd. Beautiful + banal. Intellectual + ordinary. Historical + contemporary. Real + simulated. Never choose only one side.

## 246. Central relationship to Manchester

Subject, archive, stage, laboratory, film set, memory palace, economic system, mythological landscape. Do not reduce it to backdrop.

## 247. Central relationship to night

Darkness but also illumination. Concealment but also revelation. Danger but also beauty. Loneliness but also intimacy. The contradiction is essential.

## 248. Central relationship to photography

Document, memory, evidence, fiction, surveillance, art — all at once.

## 249. Central relationship to the game

Simulation, archive, artwork, film set, delivery game, philosophical experiment, virtual Manchester, autobiographical fiction, image-making machine. Do not force it into only one category.

## 250. The long-term creative possibility

Zealot of Harpurhey could become less a completed videogame and more an expanding world — new films, new paintings, real shops that disappear and remain inside it, recurring characters, returning ideas, accumulating traces. In that sense it can itself become a spectre of Manchester: a copy, an archive, a memory, a simulation, a stage, and eventually a place from which entirely new artworks are made.

## Final creative principles

When unsure, return to these:

- The spectres are already here.
- Look at the ordinary until it becomes strange.
- The city is a text. The city is a stage. Streetlights are stage lights.
- Municipal infrastructure can be beautiful.
- Night strips the city of explanatory detail.
- Every sign carries history. Every copy changes the original. Every photograph produces another spectre.
- Meaning is unstable. Absence leaves traces. The archive is never neutral.
- The delivery bag can be armour. The courier can be a knight. The app can be an oracle. The commute can be a pilgrimage.
- The mundane can become sacred. A supermarket object can become a relic. A bus stop can become theatre. A street can become a film set.
- A game can become an archive. A virtual city can become a camera. A painting can become architecture. A T-shirt can become theory. A photograph can become a world. A world can become a film.
- Beauty does not cancel precarity. Humour does not cancel seriousness. Theory does not cancel ordinary life. Realism does not require literal accuracy.
- Surrealism should emerge from observation rather than randomness.
- Do not explain an image that already works.
- Do not transform the project into generic surrealism. Do not transform it into generic videogame design.

And perhaps most importantly: Zealot of Harpurhey should feel as though someone spent years walking through Manchester at night, photographing its lights, workers, signs, shops, trees and architecture; read Derrida, Barthes, Baudrillard, Benjamin, Cervantes, Shakespeare and Rilke; made paintings and films from what they found; experienced the economic conditions of contemporary creative life; and then rebuilt the city inside a computer so all of those things could haunt one another.
